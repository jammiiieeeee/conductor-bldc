Why it turns

Three phases, switched in order, make a magnetic field that walks around the stator. The magnets in the bell are already pointing somewhere. They chase the field, and the bell goes with them. That chase is the motor.

Copper is north at this step, ink is south. The rotor's permanent magnet turns to put its south end against a north pole. Six steps, sixty degrees each. In a real 14-pole motor the field advances seven times per shaft turn, which is why the torque stays smooth.

The nine parts

Every figure here is typical of a hobby-class outrunner 40–60 mm across. Nothing in this table is a measurement of a specific motor.

Parts of a 12-slot, 14-pole outrunner brushless motor
No.PartMaterialWhat it doesHow it fails
01 Rotor bell Anodised aluminium The spinning shell. It holds fourteen magnets at a fixed radius and carries the shaft, so the turning field has something to push against. A dent closes the air gap. Once a magnet touches the stator, the motor is scrap.
02 Magnets Sintered NdFeB, nickel-plated Fourteen poles, alternating north and south. Their fixed field is what the stator's field pushes against — no magnets, no torque. Heat. Above roughly 150 °C a NdFeB magnet loses strength permanently, and the motor loses torque with it.
03 Front bearing Steel races, steel or ceramic balls Holds the bell end of the shaft on centre and takes the radial load from the propeller or pulley. Grease cooks out, play appears, and the rotor starts to orbit instead of spin.
04 Shaft Hardened steel The only part that leaves the motor. Everything else in the stack exists to turn this. A bent shaft takes both bearings with it, and the air gap goes uneven.
05 Windings Enamelled copper Twelve coils in three phases. Current through a coil makes an electromagnet, and switching which coils are on is what moves the field. The enamel breaks down and two phases short. Usually heat, sometimes a nick during assembly.
06 Stator 0.2–0.5 mm silicon steel, laminated Gives the field a low-resistance path and aims it at the magnets. The stack is insulated so eddy currents don't turn torque into heat. Lamination insulation fails locally; that spot overheats and spreads.
07 Hall sensor board FR-4, three Hall-effect ICs Watches which magnet is passing and tells the controller. Without it, the controller is guessing when to switch phases. One dead sensor and the motor stutters, refuses to start, or runs backwards.
08 Rear bearing Steel races The second support. With the front bearing it fixes the rotor's axis — two points define a line. Usually first to go: it sits closest to the stator's heat.
09 Mount base Machined aluminium Holds the stator still and bolts the motor to the machine. The reaction torque you feel here equals the torque the shaft delivers. Stripped threads or a cracked lug — the motor turns, the machine doesn't.
